What a record contains

A consistent archival schema for each entry.
  • Coordinates: RA (h m s), Dec (° ′ ″), with epoch stated (e.g., J2000).
  • Catalog references: structured identifiers (e.g., SAO) when available.
  • Inscription: recorded name and motto, preserved as submitted.
  • Provenance: recorded-by and sponsor fields (when present).

Permanence & preservation

Stable routing + regenerable documents.
  • QR codes resolve through /r/<ID> to the canonical record at /registry/<ID>.
  • Records are published as structured JSON for long-term readability and migration safety.
  • Certificates and derived documents can be regenerated from the published entry data.

Notes on stars and naming

Short context, without mythology overload.

The night sky has been observed and recorded for millennia. Many of the star names still in use today preserve layers of historical language and scholarship: Arabic astronomers transmitted and refined much of the classical Greek knowledge, while Latinized forms later entered European star catalogues. Names such as Vega, Deneb, or Betelgeuse are reminders of this long chain of observation and translation.

Naming traditions. While traditional names remain culturally significant, modern astronomy requires unambiguous systems for identifying celestial objects. For this reason, astronomers rely primarily on catalog designations assigned through systematic surveys. Catalogues such as the SAO (Smithsonian Astrophysical Observatory), HIP (Hipparcos), and HD (Henry Draper) assign numeric identifiers to stars, ensuring that a specific object can be referenced consistently across scientific literature, star charts, and observational databases.

Continuity of record. Catalog designations, coordinates, and physical parameters together create a stable astronomical reference. They allow observations made by different instruments, observatories, and generations of astronomers to refer to the same object with precision. In this way, the scientific record of the sky remains coherent across centuries of study.

What a star is. A star is a luminous sphere of hot plasma held together by gravity and powered by nuclear fusion in its core. Within these interiors, hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium, releasing enormous amounts of energy that radiate outward as light and heat. This energy production allows stars to shine steadily for millions or billions of years depending on their mass.

Position in the sky. Astronomers locate stars using a coordinate system analogous to latitude and longitude on Earth. The position of a star is given by Right Ascension (RA) and Declination (Dec), measured relative to the celestial equator. Because the orientation of Earth’s axis changes slowly over time, these coordinates are referenced to a standard epoch, most commonly J2000.0. Including the epoch ensures that the position of the object can be reproduced accurately on maps and in digital sky viewers.

Brightness and spectral type. Stars are also characterized by measurable physical properties. Apparent magnitude describes how bright a star appears from Earth, while spectral classification reveals information about its surface temperature and composition. These classifications help astronomers understand stellar evolution, from the formation of stars within molecular clouds to their eventual transformation into white dwarfs, neutron stars, or other stellar remnants.

Magnitude scale. The brightness of a star as seen from Earth is described using the astronomical magnitude scale. This system has its origins in ancient Greek astronomy, where the brightest visible stars were described as “first magnitude” and the faintest visible stars as “sixth magnitude.” Modern astronomy preserved this terminology but redefined it using precise photometric measurements.

The magnitude scale is logarithmic rather than linear. A difference of five magnitudes corresponds to a brightness factor of exactly 100. As a result, a star that is one magnitude lower is about 2.512 times brighter than a star one magnitude higher. Because of this convention, smaller or negative magnitude values correspond to brighter objects.

Two related measurements are commonly used. Apparent magnitude describes how bright an object appears from Earth, which depends on both its intrinsic luminosity and its distance from the observer. For example, the star Vega has an apparent magnitude close to 0.0 and is easily visible to the naked eye under dark skies.

To compare the true luminosity of stars, astronomers use absolute magnitude. This value represents how bright a star would appear if it were placed at a standard distance of 10 parsecs (approximately 32.6 light-years) from Earth. By removing the effect of distance, absolute magnitude allows stars to be compared directly in terms of their intrinsic energy output.

Modern photometry measures magnitudes through specific wavelength filters, producing systems such as the UBV or Johnson–Cousins system. These measurements provide information not only about brightness but also about stellar temperature and composition, allowing astronomers to study stellar populations, evolution, and the structure of the Milky Way with quantitative precision.
